Case study · A Round Ent · consumer onboarding · 2025

A 23% lift I refused to call causal

The number was real. The claim I could make from it was much smaller, and saying so is the actual point of this case.

Problem

A dating product lives or dies in the first session

A Round builds consumer social products, including a dating app for Asian American users. In this category the economics are brutal and simple. Users decide within minutes whether to stay, and a user who never finishes onboarding is worth nothing to the matching system, because there is nothing to match on.

Our onboarding was text-heavy. It asked people to describe their preferences in writing, at the exact moment they had the least patience and the least investment.

There was also a more ordinary problem. The team had far more ideas than it could build, and no shared way to compare them.

Action

Score the ideas, ship one, then measure it honestly

1 · Turn opinions into a comparable set

I researched user behavior in this segment and reviewed more than ten competing apps, looking specifically at where each one asked for preference information and how much work it demanded from a brand-new user. That produced ten candidate opportunities.

Rather than argue them one at a time, I scored all ten on the same four dimensions and let the comparison do the work.

Match-relevance signals scored well but depended on matching logic that was not ready, so it moved later. That is dependency risk doing its job rather than a preference losing an argument.

2 · Work with the people who build it

I collected user feedback with marketing, then worked with product, design, and backend to turn recurring complaints into prioritized requirements for the login, onboarding, and preference flows. The change that shipped replaced text-entry preferences with a visual, emoji-based taxonomy, so expressing a preference became tapping rather than writing.

3 · Design the measurement before reading the result

This is the part I care most about. I defined the onboarding metrics and the analytics schema behind them before the rollout, so the analysis was not a search for a flattering cut.

  • Measured in GA4 Funnel Exploration on unique users, not event counts
  • New users only, since returning users have already completed onboarding
  • Internal test and QA traffic excluded, anomalous sessions excluded
  • Acquisition source, medium, and country mix checked across both periods, so a change in traffic composition would not masquerade as a change in behavior
  • Two equal 14-day windows, immediately before and immediately after rollout
Result

The number, and what it is worth

A relative lift of 22.7%, which rounds to 23%. Two-proportion z ≈ 1.17, two-tailed p ≈ 0.24.

So the honest reading is this. The direction is encouraging and consistent with the qualitative feedback. It is not statistically significant, and it was not a randomized test. A pre-post comparison at this sample size cannot separate the change from seasonality, traffic mix, or anything else that moved in those four weeks.

I treat it as directional evidence, not causal proof. The lift was strong enough to justify keeping the taxonomy in the MVP. It was not strong enough to claim I caused it, and I recommended a controlled experiment once traffic could support one.

  • Two features shipped into the MVP, the emoji taxonomy and localized self-expression icons
  • Substantive spec revisions on comparable work fell from roughly 3.3 rounds to 2.3, about 30% fewer, once requirements carried explicit acceptance criteria
  • A measurement standard the team kept using, defined before the result rather than after
Why this case is here

A 23% lift is the most quotable number I have, and it would be easy to put "drove a 23% increase" on a resume line and never mention the p-value. I do not, for a practical reason as much as an ethical one. Any experienced interviewer will ask how I know the change caused the lift, and the only good answer is the one where I already knew the limits before they asked. Measurement is what I studied. Overstating a measurement is a strange way to prove I understand it.
